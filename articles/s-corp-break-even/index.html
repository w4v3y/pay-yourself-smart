When Does an S-Corp Make Sense? 2026 Break-Even Math

By Josue · Updated October 27, 2026

Short answer. For a single filer paying themselves half of profit as salary, an S-Corp starts saving money at around $33,000 of net profit. But the savings only pass $3,000 a year, enough to justify running payroll and filing a corporate return, at around $110,000. The salary you can defend to the IRS moves the answer more than your profit does.

Most break-even estimates you’ll find online compare one number: the 15.3% self-employment tax on all your profit versus 15.3% payroll tax on a smaller salary. That comparison overstates the savings by roughly half, because it leaves out three costs that only show up once you’re an S-Corp.

This article walks through the full calculation for 2026, shows where the savings actually start, and explains why your salary decision matters more than any profit threshold. Every figure below comes from the same code that runs our LLC vs S-Corp calculator, so you can plug in your own numbers.

How the S-Corp saving works

As a single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietor, you pay self-employment tax on 92.35% of your net profit: 12.4% for Social Security (up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500) plus 2.9% for Medicare.

Once your LLC elects S-Corp status with Form 2553, the business pays you a W-2 salary. Only that salary carries the same 15.3% in payroll taxes (half paid by the company, half withheld from your paycheck). The rest of the profit reaches you as a distribution reported on Schedule K-1, which has no Social Security or Medicare tax.

So the saving comes from one place: the 15.3% you no longer pay on the part of profit above your salary.

Why the simple math is wrong

Take $100,000 of net profit and a $50,000 salary. The quick version says:

  • LLC self-employment tax: $14,130
  • S-Corp payroll tax on $50,000: $7,650
  • Savings: $6,480

Here is the full calculation for a single filer with no other income:

The real saving is $2,603, about 40% of the quick estimate. Three things close the gap:

  1. Running costs ($2,202). You now need payroll software (Gusto’s Simple plan is $49 a month plus $6 per person, or $660 a year for one owner), federal unemployment tax on the first $7,000 of salary, state unemployment tax, and an accountant for the S-Corp’s own return, Form 1120-S. We assume $1,200 a year of extra accounting and $300 of state unemployment tax; both vary by state and provider.
  2. A smaller QBI deduction ($1,674 more income tax). The 20% qualified business income deduction applies to business profit, and your W-2 salary doesn’t count as business income. As an LLC, the deduction here is $15,367. As an S-Corp, it falls to $8,795, so you pay more income tax.
  3. The deductions you already had. As an LLC you deduct half of your self-employment tax. As an S-Corp, the company deducts its half of payroll tax instead. The two roughly cancel, which is why the quick math seems to work until you add the first two items.

Two break-even points matter:

  • Where the S-Corp stops losing money: about $26,000 of profit with a 40% salary, $33,000 with 50% and $49,000 with 60%.
  • Where it saves $3,000 or more a year: about $70,000 with a 40% salary and $110,000 with 50%. With a 60% salary, savings stay under $2,200 across this whole range.

We use $3,000 as the “worth the hassle” line because an S-Corp adds real work: payroll every month or quarter, quarterly Form 941 filings, a separate corporate return, cleaner bookkeeping and the risk of an IRS challenge to your salary. Below that, many owners decide the paperwork isn’t worth it. Your line may be different.

Your salary matters more than your profit

Look across any row of the table: moving from a 60% to a 40% salary multiplies the savings several times over. That’s why the most important S-Corp decision is the salary, and it isn’t yours to set freely.

The IRS requires S-Corp owners who work in the business to pay themselves reasonable compensation before taking distributions. There is no percentage rule. The “60/40 rule” you may see online is a rule of thumb, not IRS guidance. The IRS looks at factors such as:

  • your training and experience,
  • your duties and how much time you put in,
  • what comparable businesses pay for similar services,
  • what you pay non-owner employees, and
  • how and when you pay yourself.

In practice, the question is what you would have to pay someone else to do your job. A solo consultant whose profit comes almost entirely from their own billable hours will have a hard time defending a salary far below market rate for that work.

A fixed salary shows the same point from the other side. If the market rate for your role is $60,000, the S-Corp’s value depends on how much profit you earn above it:

Married filing jointly

Joint filers usually see slightly higher savings at the same profit, because a lower tax bracket makes the smaller QBI deduction cost less. With a 50% salary and no other household income:

  • $100,000 of profit saves about $3,751
  • $150,000 saves about $6,235
  • $200,000 saves about $6,998

The $3,000 line arrives at around $85,000 of profit instead of $110,000. If your spouse has wages, enter them as other income in the calculator, since they push your business income into a higher bracket.

State costs can change the answer

Our figures are federal only. Some states charge S-Corps directly:

  • California taxes S-Corp income at 1.5%, with an $800 annual minimum franchise tax. A California LLC already pays an $800 annual tax, so the S-Corp only costs extra once 1.5% of its income exceeds $800. At $100,000 of profit and a $50,000 salary, the S-Corp’s income is about $44,000 and the minimum applies, so the extra cost is close to zero. At $200,000 of profit, it is roughly $550 a year.
  • Other states and cities have their own rules. New York City, for example, taxes S-Corps under its general corporation tax. Check your state’s tax agency before deciding.

State unemployment tax rates also vary widely, so replace our $300 assumption with your state’s figure.

When an S-Corp usually isn’t worth it

  • Your profit is under about $60,000, or it changes a lot from year to year.
  • A reasonable salary for your work is close to your total profit.
  • You’re in your first year and don’t know your profit yet. You can elect later.
  • Your state adds significant S-Corp taxes or fees.
  • You don’t want to run payroll and keep corporate-level books.

Frequently asked questions

Is there an IRS rule that my salary must be 60% of profit? No. The IRS requires reasonable compensation for the work you do, judged on facts like your role, experience and market pay. A fixed percentage is not a safe harbor.

Does an S-Corp lower my income tax? Usually not. It lowers Social Security and Medicare taxes. Income tax often rises slightly, because the QBI deduction only applies to the profit left after your salary.

When do I have to file Form 2553? To take effect for a given tax year, Form 2553 must be filed no more than 2 months and 15 days after that year begins, or at any time during the year before. The IRS has a relief procedure for late elections if you had reasonable cause.

What happens to my savings above $184,500 of salary? Social Security tax stops at the 2026 wage base of $184,500, for both salaries and self-employment income. If your salary would be near or above that amount, an S-Corp saves little on Social Security, and only the 2.9% Medicare tax on distributions is avoided.
